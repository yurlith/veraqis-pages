xz and bzip2 — why two rules can mean excellent recovery

xz has two classifier rules. bzip2 has two. So does nothing much, on the face of it — 7-Zip also has three, and 7-Zip is the format where damage costs you everything after it. Rule count measures how much structure can be wrong, not how much can be recovered, and these two formats are the clearest demonstration of the difference.

The word that carries the difference

The engine's rule for a failed bzip2 checksum reads: "BZIP2 block CRC mismatch". gzip's equivalent reads "Content CRC32 mismatch", singular, because a gzip has exactly one checksum covering the whole stream. That is the entire structural difference between a format you can salvage and one you cannot.

Both formats compress in independent blocks and store a check value for each. A reader that hits a bad block knows which block is bad, can skip its length, and can resume decoding at the next boundary. Nothing similar exists in gzip or in a solid 7z folder — not because their designers were careless, but because neither format defines a boundary to resume at.

"Safe optimum" is a real limit, not a hedge

Skipping a damaged block recovers the blocks around it. It does not recover the damaged block, and no amount of engineering will: the compressed bytes for that block are gone or wrong, and the check value proves it. So the ceiling for this kind of salvage is "everything except what was hit", and reaching that ceiling is the best an honest tool can do.

This is why the recoverability matrix on this site says yes for xz and bzip2 and prefix only for solid 7z. Both are statements about the format. Neither is a claim that our implementation is cleverer than someone else's.

One practical consequence: block size is a recovery decision. A bzip2 compressed with -9 uses 900 KB blocks, so one damaged block costs up to 900 KB of output. -1 uses 100 KB blocks and costs proportionally less per hit, at some ratio. Nobody chooses that setting for recovery reasons, but it is what the choice does.

What the two rules actually are

Both formats get a magic rule and a check rule. In both, only the magic rule is marked recoverable — a damaged stream header over intact compressed data is repairable; a failed check is a statement about the data itself.

Plus the fourteen rules that apply to any container. Note also LZ4 and ZSTD, which have two rules each for a different reason: there is very little in their frames that can be independently verified, which is why we abstain on them.

Honest limits

  • "9 of 10 blocks" is the headline figure from that experiment and its corpus, not a universal rate. How much survives depends on where the damage lands and how large the blocks are.
  • A .tar.xz or .tar.bz2 inherits this good behaviour, unlike .tar.gz — but the recovered blocks still contain a partial tar, which then needs tar-level salvage.
  • xz supports SHA-256 as a check as well as CRC-32/64. The engine treats a failed check the same way whichever it is; a stronger check makes the detection stronger, not the recovery.

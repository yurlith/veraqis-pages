Why one bad block need not cost the whole file

Stock xz -d abandons a whole stream on one bad block, and bzip2 stops at the first CRC failure. Yet both formats compress in independent blocks and store a checksum for each — CRC-32 or CRC-64 per block in xz, CRC-32 per block in bzip2. A reader that hits a bad block knows which block is bad, can skip it and resume at the next boundary.

The engine’s block salvage does exactly that, and keeps only blocks whose own checksum verifies again after decoding.

Salvage a damaged .xz or .bz2 in your browser

  1. Open the full engine and add the damaged .xz or .bz2 file.
  2. Open Recover → Salvage a damaged .bz2 or .xz and press Run.
  3. Download the salvaged output. Every block in it passed its own checksum; the report lists the blocks that did not.

Why gzip cannot be salvaged the same way

A .gz has exactly one CRC-32 for the whole stream, stored at the end, and no resync point. There is nothing to verify a single block against, so the engine does not claim block salvage for gzip. See gzip and tar.

Questions

Can I recover data from a corrupted .xz file?

Usually most of it. xz stores a checksum per block; the engine skips the bad block, keeps decoding, and returns every block that verifies.

What about .tar.xz or .tar.bz2?

Salvage recovers the decompressed stream block by block. The tar inside can then be checked and extracted; members inside a lost block are gone.

Does this work for gzip, lz4 or zstd?

No. gzip has one checksum for the whole stream, and lz4 and zstd blocks are not independently verifiable, so the engine abstains.

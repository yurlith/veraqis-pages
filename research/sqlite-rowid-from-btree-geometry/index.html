Recovering the identity of a deleted row without reading its content

Recovering the content of a deleted database row is the well-known problem. Recovering its identity — which row it was — is the one that decides whether the content is usable, because a value with no key belongs to no record. This measurement asked whether identity can be reconstructed from the shape of the b-tree alone, on a fixture deliberately built so the key can never be read from the content. It can: 100 of 100, exactly. The same experiment found where the method stops.

Published 4 September 2026 · EXP-SQL-IDENTITY-2026-08-23 · VERAQIS research prototype

This is a research measurement, not a shipped feature. Recovering deleted rows from free space is off by default in VERAQIS and sits behind an explicit, risk-marked forensic mode; the default recovery path ignores the freelist entirely. Nothing here changes that, and the prototype below is a research script, not the product.

Why identity is the harder half

When a row is deleted from a SQLite table, its bytes usually stay on the page. The space is marked free, and the record's payload often survives untouched until something else is written over it. Carving that payload back out is comparatively easy.

What does not survive is the front of the cell. SQLite's freeblock header is written over the beginning of the deleted cell — which is exactly where the record's length and its rowid varint were. So the usual outcome is content without a key: a row of values that cannot be placed in the table, cannot be ordered against its neighbours, and cannot be tied to a foreign key in another table.

The common workaround is to read the identity back out of the content — an id that also appears inside a text column, an application-level reference. That works, and it is worth nothing as a general result: it depends on the schema being redundant. So the fixture for this experiment was built to remove that possibility entirely.

The reconstruction never reads payload content for identity, never reads the pre-delete file's rowid values, and never consults the ground truth. Those are inputs an analyst does not have.

The method: bounds, not guesses

A rowid table's cell pointer array is ordered by ascending rowid. That single fact turns the surviving neighbours of a deleted cell into an interval that must contain the missing key.

page 12, cell pointer array after the delete
  ordinal 0   rowid 4113   ← surviving left neighbour
  ordinal 1   [ freeblock — cell deleted, rowid varint overwritten ]
  ordinal 2   rowid 4115   ← surviving right neighbour

  lower bound = left neighbour + 1 = 4114
  upper bound = right neighbour - 1 = 4114
  bounds coincide  ⇒  rowid = 4114, deterministically

when the deleted cell was first on its page there is no left
neighbour, so the bound comes from one level up:

  parent interior page separator keys
    ... | sep 4096 | child page 12 | sep 4230 | ...
  previous sibling's maximum rowid = 4096
  lower bound = 4097
  1. Confirm the deleted cell's freeblock, or the region reclaimed by the content area advancing.
  2. Walk the surviving cell pointer array on the same page and take the immediate neighbours by ordinal position, not by byte offset.
  3. Lower bound: left neighbour + 1. If there is no left neighbour, look up the parent interior page and take the previous sibling's maximum rowid, +1.
  4. Upper bound: right neighbour − 1.
  5. If the two bounds coincide, the identity is determined — not estimated.

Step 3's second half is not a detail. Before the parent separator keys were consulted, two of the hundred targets were unbounded — both were the first cell on their page, with no left neighbour to bound them. Adding one level of the tree closed both to exact bounds.

Result

On a fixture built so that no row's key is derivable from its content, all 100 deleted rows had their rowid recovered exactly: 99 derived from b-tree ordering constraints without reading any payload, and 1 read directly from a varint that physically survived. No row was left unresolved and none was reconstructed incorrectly.

Identity-recovery measurement, 2026-08-23 · fixtures noid_s0/noid_s1.sqlite, 2,000 rows, 100 stride-19 deletions · reconstruction reads the post-delete file only

The single surviving varint belongs to a physical class worth naming: sometimes a deleted cell is reclaimed by the page's content area advancing rather than by freeblocking, and then nothing is written over the record's head at all. It is rare and it is real — 1 of 100 here, 2 of 100 in an earlier fixture.

The evidence label matters as much as the count. A rowid derived this way is not observed, and it is not guessed: it is the only integer consistent with the surviving structure. That is a different class of claim from carving a value out of free space and hoping, and it is the reason the result is worth having.

Where the method stops

The stride-spaced deletions above are the easy geometry: every deleted cell keeps a surviving neighbour on each side. So a second fixture deleted adjacent rows — seven consecutive, then a pair, then a single — to force SQLite to merge the freed space.

It does merge. Each cluster produced exactly one freeblock, with one header, at the start of the merged region — 330 bytes for the seven-row cluster, matching seven cells of 55 bytes exactly. SQLite does not preserve or re-insert headers at the original cell boundaries, so the internal divisions are gone.

what the method giveson the coalesced cluster
The set of missing identitiesExact. Bounds from the previous sibling's separator key and the next surviving cell give the interval [501, 506] — six integers, exactly the six deleted rowids.
Which byte range belongs to which identityNot determined by structure. Any assignment of the six rowids to the six sub-ranges is equally consistent with b-tree ordering evidence alone.

On this fixture the sub-division happens to work — 330 / 55 = 6 exactly — but only because every cell has the same payload size. That is a property of a uniform hash-based schema, not a SQLite guarantee.

This is the finding that keeps the result honest. A solver that assumed uniform sub-division would emit assignments on a table with variable-length text as if they were determined, when in fact several permutations fit the evidence equally well. The set is exact; the assignment needs a signal the b-tree does not carry.

When adjacent rows are deleted, SQLite merges their freed space into a single freeblock with one header. Structural evidence then determines the set of missing rowids exactly — six integers for six deleted rows — but not which byte range holds which, unless every cell in the group happens to be the same size.

Identity-recovery measurement, 2026-08-23 · fixtures noid_adj_s0/noid_adj_s1.sqlite · clusters 500-506, 1000, 1500-1501 · 330-byte merged freeblock measured

One more boundary is known and untested: the method needs a surviving right neighbour. A row deleted from the end of a page, or the last row of a table, has no upper bound from this evidence, and no claim is made about it here.

What this would and would not justify shipping

Emittable, in principle

An identity whose bounds coincide is determined by the surviving structure — one integer, no alternatives. It is the kind of result the evidence model admits: unique under stated structural constraints, and falsifiable if the constraints are wrong.

Not emittable

An assignment inside a coalesced group where several permutations fit. Publishing one of them as the answer would be exactly the over-claim the gates exist to prevent — the result is a set, and it must be reported as a set.

And it stays behind the deleted-content gate regardless. A row recovered from free space carries no evidence of when it was live or whether it was ever committed, and an exactly-known identity does not change that. The identity result makes such a row placeable; it does not make it current.

Limits

  • Research prototype, not the shipped engine. The reconstruction ran as a research script; no product path was changed.
  • Single-table fixtures. Table attribution is unambiguous here by construction. A multi-table adversarial fixture was designed and not built, so nothing is claimed about attribution in general.
  • Deletions were never the last cell on a page, so the no-right-neighbour case is untested rather than solved.
  • Uniform payload sizes in the coalesced fixture. That is what made the sub-division arithmetic work, and it is why the sub-division is reported as fixture-specific rather than as a result.
  • Two fixtures, 2,000 rows each. A measurement of a mechanism, not a survey of real databases.

Questions about deleted rows and identity

Can a deleted SQLite row be identified after its rowid is overwritten?

Often, yes — and without reading its content. SQLite orders a table's cell pointer array by ascending rowid, so the surviving neighbours of a deleted cell bound the missing key. When the bounds coincide the identity is determined exactly. In this measurement that resolved 99 of 100 cases, with the hundredth recovered from a varint that physically survived.

Why is the rowid lost when the row data survives?

Because SQLite writes the freeblock header over the start of the deleted cell, which is where the record length and the rowid varint live. The payload further into the cell is usually untouched, so content commonly outlives identity.

What happens when several adjacent rows are deleted?

SQLite merges the freed space into one freeblock with a single header, so the internal cell boundaries are gone. Structure still determines the set of missing rowids exactly, but not which byte range holds which — unless every cell in the group is the same size, which is a property of the data rather than of SQLite.

Does VERAQIS recover deleted rows?

Not by default. Recovery ignores the freelist, and resurrecting deleted content is a separate explicit mode marked as risky. Rows are recovered through the transactional record instead — from a write-ahead log, for example — where there is evidence of when a row was live and whether it was committed.

Is this method reliable enough to use as evidence?

The identity part is deterministic where the bounds coincide: one integer is consistent with the surviving structure and no other is. The parts that are not deterministic are named here rather than smoothed over — a coalesced group yields a set, not an assignment, and a row deleted from the end of a page has no upper bound at all.

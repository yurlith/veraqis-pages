Recovering past a corrupted block in xz and bzip2

Compressed formats fail loudly and completely: one bad block and xz -d reports "Compressed data is corrupt" and hands you nothing. But xz and bzip2 compress in independent blocks, each with its own checksum. That structure is recoverable — and unlike most recovery, every recovered byte comes with proof that is not the decoder's own opinion.

Published 18 July 2026 · Updated 28 July 2026 · EXP-PDS · VERAQIS 0.2.1

Why stock tools give up

A decompressor has one job and a strict contract: produce the original bytes or fail. When it meets a corrupt block it cannot honour that contract, so it stops. That is correct behaviour for a decompressor and terrible behaviour for someone holding their only copy of a backup.

The opportunity is that xz and bzip2 are block-independent. Each block is compressed on its own and carries its own check value — CRC-32 or CRC-64 in xz, CRC-32/BZIP2 in bzip2. Corruption inside one block says nothing about the others. A tool willing to resynchronise past the damaged block can decode the rest, and can prove each one it emits.

That last part is what separates this from "recover as much as possible". The check value covering each block was not used to construct the block — it was used to check it after decoding. That independence is the whole difference between evidence and a tautology.

The headline case

A real 10-block xz stream with mid-stream corruption, run through both the stock tool and ours:

xz -d (stock)

Fails the whole stream with "Compressed data is corrupt". Roughly 3 blocks of output before it stops, and it is output the tool does not stand behind.

phx recover --salvage

Recovers 9 of 10 blocks, resynchronising past the corrupted one. Each block carries 64 bits of independent CRC-64 evidence. false_recovered_bytes = 0.

On a real 10-block xz stream corrupted mid-stream, stock xz -d abandons the entire stream, while block-independent salvage recovers 9 of the 10 blocks — each re-verified by its own CRC-64, 64 bits of evidence independent of the decode, with zero false recovered bytes.

Full measurement

Yield here means recovered-useful ÷ recoverable-given-the-damage — not a share of the original file. A truncated stream cannot yield bytes that were never written; the question is whether we get everything that survived. Each cell is 24 damage positions, and we gate on the lower bound of the 95% Wilson interval rather than the point estimate.

N = 24 damage positions per cell. false_recovered_bytes is a hard gate at 0, and it holds by construction: a block is emitted only after its own check value verifies.

The hard case: xz truncation

xz keeps its Index — the table giving every block's position and size — at the end of the stream. Truncate the file and the Index is the first thing you lose, which is exactly when you need it.

For threaded xz (-T0, the common default for large files) the block headers themselves carry compressed and uncompressed sizes. That makes an Index-free forward walk possible: start at the stream header, read each block header, verify the block-header CRC-32, jump by the declared size, decode, and verify the block check. Every complete block before the cut is relocated and emitted with proof.

Single-block streams and xz -T1 truncation abstain. Those headers omit the sizes, so the block cannot be bounded without decoding it — and a decode-until-error prefix is not verifiable. We report the limit instead of emitting bytes we cannot stand behind. That is a decoder limit, not a false byte.

Where we deliberately refuse

lz4 and zstd are recognised, and then abstained. Both are LZ-coupled: the compressed stream carries no independent per-block verifier, so there is no way to check a recovered region against anything the recovery did not itself produce. We could emit plausible bytes. We would have no way to tell you which of them are right, and false_recovered_bytes > 0 is not a trade we make.

For lz4 and mid-stream zstd, VERAQIS detects the format and then abstains. No independent per-block verifier exists in those streams, so emitted bytes could not be checked against anything other than the process that produced them.

What you get back

phx recover damaged.xz --salvage --output out/

out/salvage.phxr.json          # codec, per-block offset/length/SHA-256, evidence per block
out/salvage/block_0000.bin     # recovered block bytes, each CRC-verified
out/payload.bin                # written ONLY when recovered blocks tile the whole
                               # output with known offsets and no gap

The last line is a deliberate restriction. A single joined payload is produced only when the recovered blocks form a complete, gap-free cover with known offsets — otherwise you get the blocks separately. Concatenating blocks across a gap would silently shift every byte after it, producing a file that looks whole and is wrong.

The same care applies to offsets. xz's Index gives every block's uncompressed offset even across a gap, so placement is known exactly. After a bzip2 gap the offset is reported as unknown — the bytes are correct, but where they belong in the original is not something we will invent.

Limits

  • The corrupted block itself is not recovered. Salvage recovers around damage, never through it.
  • gzip, DEFLATE and tar are handled by a different mechanism (member and continuation resync) because they are not block-independent in this sense.
  • Every decode is output-capped as a decompression-bomb guard, so a hostile stream cannot turn recovery into a denial-of-service.
  • Yield is measured against what survived the damage, not against the original file. A file truncated to half its size cannot yield more than half.
  • The CRC evidence here is accidental-grade. It establishes that these are the bytes that were compressed, not that nobody chose them — see the CRC-32 forgery measurement.

Practical takeaway

If you compress large archival data and might one day need to recover part of it, the block structure of your format decides what is possible. xz -T0 and bzip2 leave a recovery surface with independent proof on every block. zstd and lz4 are faster and compress well, but a damaged stream leaves nothing that can be independently verified.

That is a real trade-off worth making deliberately rather than discovering afterwards.

Questions about compressed-stream recovery

Can data be recovered from a corrupted xz file?

Usually yes, apart from the damaged block itself. xz compresses in independent blocks, each with its own CRC, so a tool that resynchronises past the corrupt block can decode and verify the rest. In our measurement a 10-block stream with mid-stream corruption yielded 9 of 10 blocks, while stock xz -d abandoned the whole stream.

Why does xz -d refuse to output anything?

A decompressor guarantees exact output. Once it hits a corrupt block it cannot keep that guarantee, so it stops rather than emit bytes it cannot stand behind. Salvage is a different job with a different contract: recover the blocks that verify independently, and be explicit about the rest.

Can a truncated xz file be recovered?

If it was compressed with threaded xz (-T0), yes: the block headers carry their own sizes, so every complete block before the cut can be located and verified without the Index that truncation removed. Single-block streams and xz -T1 omit those sizes, and there VERAQIS abstains rather than guess.

What about zstd and lz4?

They are detected and then abstained. Neither carries an independent per-block verifier, so recovered bytes could not be checked against anything other than the recovery process itself. Emitting them would mean claiming a correctness we cannot demonstrate.

Is bzip2 more recoverable than xz?

Both reach the safe optimum in our measurements for truncation and mid-stream corruption. bzip2 has the simpler story because its per-block CRC and block structure are self-contained; xz needs the threaded layout for the truncation case, but offers CRC-64 evidence where bzip2 offers CRC-32.

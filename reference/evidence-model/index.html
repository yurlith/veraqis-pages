The evidence model

Recovery tools usually have one dial: a confidence number. That collapses two different kinds of certainty into one scale where they cannot be compared. VERAQIS keeps them apart, counts one of them in bits, and refuses to count evidence the solver produced for itself.

Published 28 July 2026

Why one confidence number is not enough

Consider two recovered items. The first is a file whose CRC-32 matched after repair. The second is a database row that fits exactly one recovered schema and no other. Both feel like "high confidence" — but they can fail in completely different ways.

The first can fail by collision, and the chance of that is calculable: about one in four billion for a random error. The second cannot fail by collision at all; it fails if our model of the format is incomplete — if some structure exists that we did not consider. There is no honest number for that, only an empirical record of how often it has been wrong on a corpus with known answers.

Averaging those into one score destroys the information a careful reader needs. So they stay separate, permanently.

Axis P — counted in bits

Independent checksums and hashes. Fails by collision, at a rate you can bound. CRC-32 = 32 bits; a cryptographic hash over the emitted region = 256.

Axis L — no bits, ever

Structural uniqueness. Fails by model incompleteness, not collision, so any bit value would be fiction. Validated by a zero-false record on a differential corpus.

Axis S — a suggestion

Synthesised content that makes a file open. Never verified, never raises health, always labelled.

The tautology rule

This is the rule that does the most work, and the one most often missing elsewhere.

A checksum that the solver targeted contributes zero evidence bits. If a repair was constructed to make a CRC match, that CRC matching afterwards tells you nothing — a full-rank solve satisfies it by construction. Only a verifier from outside the solved-against set can confirm the result.

VERAQIS evidence model, governing rule. Enforced by a machine-checked emission invariant and by the certificate verifier, which rejects tautology smuggling.

It is easy to violate this accidentally and it looks like success while you do. A tool reconstructs a damaged region by solving for the bytes that make the stored checksum match, then reports "checksum verified". The check is real; the inference is empty. The tool has verified its own arithmetic.

Which is why every VERAQIS result records two separate lists: independent_verifiers — what was actually checked — and solved_against — what was targeted and therefore counts for nothing. A certificate that tries to move an entry from the second list to the first is rejected by the standalone verifier; that specific forgery is one of the seven we test against ourselves.

When a health score is allowed to improve

A recovery tool that raises its own health score on the strength of guessed bytes is grading its own homework. The rule is narrow on purpose. Health may increase only from:

  • an exact erasure resolved against a trusted, corroborated checksum; or
  • Axis-P evidence of at least 32 bits with at least one independent verifier; or
  • Axis-L structural uniqueness that passed its uniqueness gate with a clean differential record.

It may never increase from zero evidence bits, from a merely-best structural guess, or from a suggestion. Inferred bytes never move the score. Neither do bytes justified only by a checksum that was solved against.

Two threat models, kept apart

The same evidence means different things depending on who you are defending against, so a claim whose meaning changes between the two must say which one it answers.

Accidental damageAdversarial tampering
The questionDid a bit flip, a truncated download or a bad sector change these bytes?Could someone have deliberately substituted these bytes?
CRC-class evidenceSufficient — about 32 bitsNever sufficient — CRC-32 is forgeable by direct construction
What is requiredAn independent checksum the solver did not targetA cryptographic witness that existed before the repair

Anti-self-witness. Evidence regenerated from the repaired file cannot witness that file's own correctness. A checksum recomputed after the repair, a parser that now opens the file, or metadata rebuilt from the candidate all prove that the repair is self-consistent — never that it is right.

An unlabelled result defaults to the weaker, accidental reading. Silence never upgrades a claim.

Definitions

Axis P — probabilistic evidence

Evidence quantified in bits: evidence_bits = −log₂(upper bound on P(false accept)), summed over verifiers that are mutually independent and independent of whatever produced the candidate.

CRC-32 = 32 bits · a second independent CRC-32 = +32 · Adler-32 ≈ 32 · a cryptographic hash over the exact emitted region = 256.

Axis L — logical / structural evidence

“Unique under the stated structural constraints.” Two sub-classes: structural_unique (provably one candidate) and structural_heuristic (the best candidate, not a unique one). The second never raises health.

Gets no bit value, because its failure mode is model incompleteness rather than collision. Validated empirically by a zero-false record on a differential corpus.

Axis S — suggestion

Synthesised content offered so a file will open. Never verified, never raises health, always labelled as a suggestion.

evidence_bits

The summed width of every independent verifier that checked an emitted region. Zero bits with no exact-erasure basis forbids emission entirely.

Tautology rule

A checksum the solver targeted contributes 0 bits. The confirming verifier must come from outside the solved-against set.

Exact erasure

The damage is known to be an erasure — we know which bytes are gone, not merely that something is wrong — and a trusted checksum covers the gap with full rank. The solution is then the unique original, deterministically.

false_recovered_bytes

Emitted bytes that differ from the original. A hard gate fixed at zero on every recovery layer. A method that returns more data at the cost of one wrong byte does not ship.

Abstention

Returning unknown is a legal, and often correct, output. Abstention is not failure; an unlabelled guess is.

Questions about the evidence model

What are evidence bits?

A count of how much independent checking a recovered region received, expressed as the negative log base 2 of an upper bound on the chance of accepting something wrong. A verified CRC-32 is 32 bits; a cryptographic hash over exactly the emitted region is 256. Only verifiers independent of the process that produced the candidate may be counted.

What is the tautology rule?

If a solver constructed a candidate specifically so that a checksum would match, that checksum matching afterwards proves nothing — the solve satisfies it by construction. Such a checksum contributes zero evidence bits, and a result must be confirmed by a verifier from outside the set it was solved against.

What is the difference between recovered and verified data?

Recovered means bytes were produced. Verified means an independent check confirmed them. VERAQIS keeps these separate in every report: verified entries carry their evidence, recovered-but-unverified entries are labelled as such, and unknown regions stay unknown.

Why does structural evidence get no bit value?

Because it does not fail by collision. A structurally unique candidate is wrong only if our model of the format missed a possibility, and there is no principled number for that. Instead it is held to an empirical standard: zero false results on a corpus where the right answer is known.
